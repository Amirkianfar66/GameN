The same filter over four pictures from two very different sources. One is your painted Room A picture; three are my code-drawn plates. What the look does, in order

  1. Exposure matched: every source lands on the same darks, mid-tones and lights, so a bright painting and a dark drawing start equal.
  2. Team colours kept away: blues go to teal, reds to amber, violets to magenta. Room A's purple doorway moved from 31 to 70 away from the Alien purple. All four results are at least 39.9 from every team colour.
  3. Colour script: one limited palette per location (steel, rust, cosmos). Only the glowing accents keep full colour.
  4. Noir contrast: shadows crushed toward the location's black, with hard light.
  5. Comic tones: flat bands, halftone dots in the shadows, and an ink line drawn along strong edges.
  6. Light and print: bloom on the accents, haze by depth, grain, specks and a vignette.

Three layers, one picture

  • Same steps, tuned by depth. The far layer gets less contrast, haze and softness. The middle gets full contrast. The foreground gets the deepest blacks and heaviest ink, slightly out of focus. That difference is what makes three flat images feel deep.
  • The filter finishes the match; it can't create it. Perspective, light direction and scale must already agree, which is why the three prompts share one camera, one light and one style block.

On the phone

  • Each background is filtered once when the images are prepared, never on the phone. This prototype takes about 1.6 s a picture on this machine.
  • Mira's drawings get the same grade when they're drawn at load.
  • Only grain, halftone and vignette run every frame, as screen layers, the way the boarding film already does them. That engine avoids per-frame canvas filters because they're slow on phones.
